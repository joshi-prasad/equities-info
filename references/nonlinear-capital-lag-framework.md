
# Nonlinear Capital-Lag Framework

The **Nonlinear Capital-Lag Framework** is a way of thinking about companies where **capital is invested today, but the economic returns from that capital arrive much later—and often not smoothly**.

The key idea is:

> **Capital creates the capacity to earn; time, execution, and demand determine when that capacity actually earns.**

That produces the **J-shaped return curve** shown in your image.

### 1. The basic idea

Imagine a company spends ₹100 crore building a new manufacturing facility.

Initially:

**₹100 crore invested → little revenue → depreciation + interest + startup costs**

So returns on capital can actually **fall below the company's cost of capital**.

Then the plant goes through:

**Construction → qualification → customer approval → production ramp-up → utilisation → operating leverage**

Once utilisation rises, the same ₹100 crore of capital can generate much more revenue and profit.

The exact shape isn't important—the concept is that **returns can initially deteriorate and then improve sharply once the capital starts working**.

---

## 2. Why is it called "non-linear"?

A normal capital-investment assumption might implicitly look like:

> Invest ₹100 → returns gradually increase → returns remain fairly stable.

But manufacturing businesses often behave differently.

For example:

| Stage         |      Capital invested | Utilisation |           Returns |
| ------------- | --------------------: | ----------: | ----------------: |
| Build         |                  High |       0–20% |         Very poor |
| Qualification |                  High |      20–40% |              Poor |
| Ramp-up       |          Same capital |      40–70% | Improving rapidly |
| Harvest       |          Same capital |      70–90% |       Very strong |
| Mature        | More capital required |        90%+ |       May flatten |

The important point is that **₹100 crore of additional capital doesn't necessarily produce ₹X of profit immediately**.

There is a **capital lag**.

---

## 3. The four phases in your diagram

The image divides the J-curve into four stages.

### Phase 1 — Early investment

**Returns < Cost of Capital**

The company is spending money but hasn't yet generated sufficient earnings.

Typical reasons:

* new plant under construction
* machinery depreciation starts
* employees hired before revenue arrives
* customer qualification costs
* working capital builds
* interest expense
* low utilisation

This can make a company temporarily look worse financially.

---

### Phase 2 — Gates / Ramp-up

This is the **most interesting part for an investor**.

The capital has been deployed, but the company is waiting for:

* validation
* customer qualification
* PPAP/FAI
* production approval
* customer onboarding
* volume ramp-up

Once these gates are crossed, revenue can start accelerating **without proportional new capital expenditure**.

That's where operating leverage appears.

---

### Phase 3 — Harvest

Now utilisation rises.

Suppose the plant can eventually produce ₹500 crore of revenue but initially produces only ₹100 crore.

If the majority of fixed costs have already been incurred, going from:

**₹100 crore → ₹300 crore → ₹500 crore**

can produce a disproportionately large increase in EBITDA.

This is the **non-linearity**.

The capital was invested earlier; the earnings arrive later.

---

### Phase 4 — Mature / Congestion

Eventually, the plant becomes highly utilised.

Now another problem appears:

> **You need the next round of capital to continue growing.**

The existing plant may be near capacity.

Therefore:

**High utilisation → new capex → another capital lag → another J-curve**

This is why the diagram says:

> "Returns flatten, next cycle needed."

---

## 4. The really important concept: the "capital clock"

This is probably the most useful part of the framework.

Different businesses have **different capital clocks**.

For example:

### A CRO

Capital may need to be continually refreshed.

So:

**Investment → utilisation → replacement → investment → utilisation...**

There may never be a large harvesting period.

### A specialised manufacturing plant

Could look like:

**₹500 Cr capex → 2 years qualification/ramp → 5 years high utilisation → next plant**

This can produce a much more attractive J-curve.

### A mature pharma manufacturing facility

If the plant is already fully utilised, the question changes from:

> "Will this capital earn?"

to:

> **"How much am I paying for the cash flows this mature asset generates?"**

That's why the image puts Divi's near the mature end.

---

## 5. Why this is useful for investing

This framework prevents a common mistake:

### Looking only at current ROCE/ROIC.

Suppose Company A has:

**ROCE = 25%**

and Company B has:

**ROCE = 12%**

At first glance, A looks better.

But suppose:

### Company A

Existing assets are mature and generating 25% ROCE, but the company has no major growth runway.

### Company B

Just invested heavily in a new facility. Current ROCE is temporarily 12%, but the facility could reach 30%+ ROCE after qualification and utilisation.

The **current ROCE doesn't tell you where the company is on its capital clock**.

That's the central insight.

## 8. The investor's key question

The framework ultimately changes the question from:

> **"Is this company expensive or cheap based on today's earnings?"**

to:

> **"What has already been invested, how long until that capital reaches productive utilisation, and what returns can that capital generate once it does?"**

And then:

> **"How much additional capital is required to reach the next J-curve?"**

That last question is crucial because **growth isn't valuable simply because revenue grows**.

If a company has to continuously invest ₹100 to generate only ₹105 of incremental earnings, growth can destroy value.

If ₹100 of capital eventually produces ₹125–₹150+ of incremental earnings/cash flow, the reinvestment can become extremely valuable.

---

### A simple mental model

When analysing a company, I would use these **5 questions**:

1. **How much capital has already been committed?**
2. **What is the current utilisation of that capital?**
3. **What gates remain before full commercial ramp-up?**
4. **What incremental ROIC/ROCE can the asset generate at maturity?**
5. **How much new capital will be needed to sustain the next growth cycle?**

That is essentially the **Nonlinear Capital-Lag Framework** in practical investing language.

And the most important insight from the image is the sentence at the bottom:

> **"Capital creates the option; demand decides whether it pays."**

In other words, **capex creates capacity, but only actual customer demand and utilisation turn that capacity into attractive returns.**

## Your framework, sharpened

### 1. What is the **existing capacity**?

Understand:

* Current installed capacity
* Current utilisation
* Product mix
* Revenue generated at current utilisation
* Whether the constraint is actually manufacturing capacity

For example:

> Capacity = 100 units
> Current utilisation = 70%
> Current revenue = ₹700 Cr

That gives you a baseline.

---

### 2. What **new capacity** is being created?

Identify:

* Capex already spent
* Capex still to be spent
* Capacity addition
* Expected commissioning date
* Whether the company has built ahead of demand or against confirmed orders

This is where you start the **capital clock**.

---

### 3. What are the **commercial gates**?

This is extremely important, particularly for businesses such as aerospace, defence, semiconductor, medical devices, specialised auto components, etc.

Map:

**Plant construction**

↓

**Equipment installation**

↓

**Trial production**

↓

**Certification / accreditation**

↓

**Customer qualification**

↓

**PPAP / FAI / validation**

↓

**First commercial production**

↓

**Volume ramp-up**

The plant being physically complete does **not** mean the capital is economically productive.

---

### 4. What is the **demand visibility**?

Now connect the capacity to actual demand.

Look for:

* Order book
* Long-term contracts
* RFQs
* Customer nominations
* Won business
* SOP dates
* Existing customer expansion
* Management's revenue targets

This is where your sentence:

> **"Capital creates the option; demand decides whether it pays."**

becomes very practical.

A ₹500 Cr plant with no customer demand is very different from a ₹500 Cr plant backed by ₹1,000 Cr of executable orders.

---

### 5. How long will the **commercial ramp-up** take?

This is the **capital-lag period**.

For example:

| Year | Capacity | Utilisation |
| ---- | -------: | ----------: |
| FY27 |      100 |         20% |
| FY28 |      100 |         40% |
| FY29 |      100 |         65% |
| FY30 |      100 |         85% |
| FY31 |      100 |         90% |

This tells you something that a five-year revenue CAGR completely hides:

**the company may have already spent the money, while the earnings are still several years away.**

---

## 6. How much revenue can the asset generate at full utilisation?

This is one of the most important calculations.

Suppose:

**New capacity = 100 units**

and:

**Revenue per unit = ₹10 lakh**

Then theoretical revenue capacity is:

**100 × ₹10 lakh = ₹100 Cr**

But don't automatically use ₹100 Cr.

You need to understand:

* product mix
* yield
* scrap
* realisation
* customer concentration
* actual sustainable utilisation

So you might conclude:

> Theoretical capacity = ₹100 Cr
> Sustainable revenue at 85–90% utilisation = ₹85–90 Cr

That becomes the **revenue ceiling of the current capital cycle**.

# 7. Then comes the part you added: **incremental costs**

This is where the framework becomes much more powerful.

Don't ask only:

> "How much revenue will the new plant generate?"

Ask:

> **"What does it cost to generate each additional ₹100 of revenue?"**

Break costs into:

### Variable costs

* raw materials
* power
* consumables
* freight
* variable labour
* subcontracting

### Semi-variable costs

* supervisors
* maintenance
* quality personnel
* logistics infrastructure

### Fixed costs

* depreciation
* plant overhead
* management
* administrative expenses

The key is that **many costs don't rise proportionately with revenue**.

That's where operating leverage comes from.

---

For example:

Existing business:

**Revenue = ₹500 Cr**
**EBITDA = ₹100 Cr**

New capacity produces:

**Additional revenue = ₹200 Cr**

Suppose incremental costs are ₹130 Cr.

Then:

**Incremental EBITDA = ₹70 Cr**

So:

**Incremental EBITDA margin = 35%**

That's much more informative than simply looking at consolidated EBITDA margin.

# 9. Then go one level deeper: incremental ROCE

This is where I would slightly modify your point **g**.

Don't calculate:

> Incremental ROCE = incremental revenue / incremental capital

Instead calculate approximately:

$$
\text{Incremental ROCE}
=
\frac{\Delta NOPAT}
{\Delta Invested\ Capital}
$$

Where:

**ΔNOPAT = incremental EBIT × (1 − tax rate)**

And incremental invested capital should include the capital actually required to support the growth:

**Growth capex + incremental working capital + other operating capital**

So imagine:

Additional capex = ₹200 Cr
Additional working capital = ₹30 Cr

Total incremental capital = **₹230 Cr**

Incremental EBIT = ₹60 Cr
Tax = 25%

NOPAT = ₹45 Cr

Therefore:

$$
Incremental\ ROCE =
\frac{45}{230}
=19.6\%
$$

Now you've got something economically meaningful.

---

# 10. And there's one more question I would add: **How much reinvestment is required after this?**

This is the part that connects directly to your preferred **reinvestment-runway approach**.

Suppose:

### Cycle 1

₹200 Cr capital → ₹45 Cr NOPAT

That's ~22.5% incremental return before considering working capital, etc.

But then the company says:

> "To grow revenue another ₹200 Cr, we need another ₹250 Cr of capex."

Now you need to ask whether the next investment produces a similar return.

So the real pattern becomes:

**Capital → lag → ramp → utilisation → cash flow → ROIC → reinvestment → next cycle**

This is much more powerful than a simple DCF assumption such as:

> Revenue grows 20% for five years.

### And then add one final question:

> **"What could prevent the J-curve from happening?"**

This is your **bear-case analysis**.

For example:

* qualification delayed
* customer delays SOP
* order gets cancelled
* utilisation remains low
* pricing pressure

> **Capital has been deployed → demand is visible → commercial gates are being crossed → utilisation should rise → incremental margins should expand → incremental ROCE should exceed the cost of capital → sufficient cash should be generated to fund the next growth cycle.**

That, in my view, is a **far stronger way to study a capital-intensive growth company** than simply projecting revenue and applying a terminal multiple.
