This is essentially a **“price can compensate for almost everything except integrity”** investing principle.

The quote is making three related points:

1. **A bad-looking business can sometimes be a good investment at the right price.**
   Low growth, cyclicality, temporary margin pressure, high capex, or even mediocre economics may be tolerable if the market price is sufficiently low.

2. **Promoter integrity is different.**
   If management/promoters cannot be trusted, a low valuation may not provide the same margin of safety. The reported earnings, cash flows, assets, related-party transactions, capital allocation, etc. themselves become questionable.

3. **The “zero” analogy is powerful.**
   Mathematically:

   $$
   \text{Anything} \times 0 = 0
   $$

   So the argument is that:

   $$
   \text{Business quality} \times \text{valuation} \times \text{management integrity}
   $$

   can be thought of conceptually as having an integrity factor. If that factor approaches zero, the other attractive characteristics don't necessarily save the investment.

### The important nuance

I would **not** interpret this as saying *“buy any decent business if it becomes cheap.”*

There are at least three separate questions:

* **Is the business economically sound?**
* **Is the price sufficiently below reasonable intrinsic value?**
* **Can I trust the people allocating capital and reporting the numbers?**

The first two can be modeled to some extent. The third requires a different kind of diligence: promoter pledging, related-party transactions, auditor history, capital allocation, dilution, acquisitions, governance record, disclosures, and consistency between what management says and what it subsequently does.

In fact, for a long-term investor, **integrity is partly a prerequisite for believing the financial model itself**. If the underlying numbers cannot be trusted, even a sophisticated DCF or reinvestment analysis can produce a false sense of precision.

The phrase **“forced to ignore what can be ignored”** is also interesting. It doesn't mean ignoring everything negative. It means distinguishing between **priced-in imperfections** and **unacceptable risks that invalidate the investment thesis**.
